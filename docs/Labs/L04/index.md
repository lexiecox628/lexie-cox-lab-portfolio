# A4 – Benchmark a Parameter

## Parameter

I chose the horizontal bridges. I predict that as the gap between the supports gets further apart the print will fail and the bridge will either sink or the printer will not know what to do and the filament will fall. I predict that this will happen in the 15 mm gap and 20 mm gap I made making the limit around 10 mm.

## Lessons Learned

The outcome was different than what I predicted. Instead of the print failing at 15 mm it did not fail at all where I tested. However the bottoms of the bridges of the 15 and 20 mm gaps do have a slight curve to them and the 5 and 10 mm look better. This is a very slight thing I noticed and had to really look and feel the bridges to notice. In this way the Core One printers exceed the the class design rules chart. 

One lesson I learned was that the Prusa Core One's uses a cooling technique so that the filament stays in the middle of the bridge rather than sagging or falling. I also think that using PLA rather than PETG helped with this because PLA does not need to be heated up to the point that PETG needs to. This allows the span gaps to be wider than I originally thought. I also learned that the design guidelines are conservative so that it you know the print will not fail and to have more of a factor of safety. This means that you can make bridges span more but it is not recommended. Another thing I learned about 3D printing bridges is that the support walls have to be strong and is crucial to how the bridge will turn out. In my design I used a honeycomb infill design to make it stronger and I also made the fill density a little bigger so that the supports will be anchored down. After doing some research I also found that the speed can affect the bridges span. Meaning that if the 3D printer was going too slow the filament would just fall when making the initial span and if it was going too fast it would break.
